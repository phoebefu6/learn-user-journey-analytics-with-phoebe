Part 1 · covers the GA4 checkout journey report

Inside the last gate: three steps 8 min live

GA4's checkout journey report opens the gate up as a closed funnel: begin_checkout → add_shipping_info → add_payment_info → purchase, with abandonment shown below each step. Mango Lane's month: 16,200 → 14,580 (90%) → 12,150 (83.3%) → 9,720 (80%). Multiply the chain: 0.90 x 0.833 x 0.80 = 0.60 - exactly the 60% completion the metrics course reported as one number.

LiveCart abandonment vs checkout abandonment - stop mixing them4 min▶

Baymard's famous 70.22% is CART abandonment - everyone who added to cart and did not buy - and roughly 42-43% of those people were just browsing with no intent to buy that day. Mostly normal shopper behavior. The micro-funnel above measures checkout abandonment: people who started entering shipping details and quit. That 40% loss (16,200 → 9,720) is the fixable slice - these shoppers proved intent by starting to pay.

  • Cart abandonment - big, partly normal, watch the trend not the level.
  • Checkout abandonment - smaller, mostly actionable, diagnose per step.

LiveForm-field forensics4 min▶

Baymard's 2024 benchmark: the average US checkout runs 5.1 steps and shows 11.3 form fields - while an ideal checkout needs only about 8 fields. Their key finding: field count predicts friction better than step count - a 5-step flow with 8 fields beats a 3-step flow with 14. The "too long or complicated" reason (17%) is usually a field problem wearing a step costume.

The wallet effect Wallet payments (Apple Pay, Google Pay, PayPal) collapse the payment step's fields to nearly zero and carry their own trust - one mechanism that attacks the 19% card-trust reason and the field count at once. This is why "one-page checkout + wallets" was b4's completion lever.
Self-studyFriction vs reassurance - the measured trade3 min read▶

Shorter is not automatically better. The card-trust reason (19%) means some ADDITIONS help: security badges near the card field, a visible returns link (13% cite returns policy), an order summary that keeps the total honest (12% cite hidden totals). The discipline: every added element must answer a named Baymard reason, and every removal must target the field count. "Cleaner" is not a reason; "attacks the 18% account-wall loss" is.

Also policy beats pixels at the top of the table: extra costs (40%) and slow delivery (20%) are business decisions - free-shipping thresholds, carrier choices - not UI work. Half of checkout optimization is a pricing meeting.

Demo · everyone computes

Locate and price a drop ★ 15 min · everyone works

Three drills with full arithmetic - the per-step chain is small enough to run on paper.

Verify the chain. 16,200 x 0.90 = 14,580. 14,580 x 0.8333 = 12,150. 12,150 x 0.80 = 9,720. And 0.90 x 0.8333 x 0.80 = 0.60 - the chain reproduces the metrics course's completion exactly. Cross-footing is the habit; a chain that does not multiply back is a tracking bug, not a business event.

Locate a drop. This week completion falls to 54%. The per-step read: 16,200 → 14,580 (90%, held) → 12,150 (83.3%, held) → 8,748. The last step fell from 80% to 8,748 ÷ 12,150 = 72%. The leak has an address: the payment step. Suspects, straight off the Baymard map: declined cards (a provider issue?), a payment-method outage, or a trust regression (new layout burying the security cues). Nobody redesigns the shipping form today.

Price a fix. Wallets lift the payment step from 80% to 86%: orders = 16,200 x 0.90 x 0.8333 x 0.86 = 10,449. That is +729 orders, +7.5% - the same rung you saw in b4's ladder, now derived from inside the gate. At $68 AOV: about +$49,600 GMV per month on current traffic - the one-line business case.

Real world

Why per-step beats per-gate in a war room. "Completion fell" starts a blame meeting between UX, payments, and shipping. "The payment step fell from 80% to 72% at 2pm Tuesday, shipping step flat" ends it - payments owns the next hour, everyone else goes back to work. The micro-funnel is an argument-compression device.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Completion falls from 60% to 54%; shipping and payment-entry steps held at 90% and 83.3%. Where is the leak?

The chain multiplies: if two steps held, the third must account for the drop. 0.54 ÷ (0.90 x 0.8333) = 0.72. Per-step data turns "completion fell" into an address.

2 · A card-decline spike at the payment provider hits which reason and step?

Declines bite between entering payment details and completing purchase. A sudden fall isolated to that step with UX unchanged points at the provider before the pixels.

3 · Baymard's field research says the better friction predictor is...

Fields, not steps, are where shoppers give up. "Too long or complicated" (17%) is usually excess fields; wallets attack the count and the trust reason at once.
