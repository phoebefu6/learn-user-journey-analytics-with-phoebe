The last mile

16,200 Mango Lane sessions start checkout every month. 9,720 pay. The 6,480 who quit mid-payment are the most expensive people in your business - they chose a product, tapped the buy button, and then changed their minds while holding out a card. This session teaches you to read the last mile like a leader: which abandonment numbers are normal, which are fixable, and why half the fixes are policy decisions only you can make.

Part 1 · the distinction that stops bad meetings

The last 16,200: two abandonments, two problems 10 min read

Mango Lane's last mile: 16,200 sessions begin checkout, 9,720 pay - a 60% completion rate, genuinely strong against the ~45% typical for comparable stores. But the meeting that goes wrong starts one screen earlier, when someone projects "70% of carts are abandoned!" and the room panics. That number mixes two populations that need opposite responses.

LiveThe distinction that stops bad meetings4 min▶

Two terms, two populations, two responses:

  • Cart abandonment - someone added a product and never bought. Baymard Institute's average across 50 studies is 70.22% (2025 survey wave). Sounds catastrophic, but when Baymard asks abandoners why, roughly 42-43% say they were just browsing - comparing, saving for later, window shopping. A large share of cart abandonment is normal shopper behavior, not a broken store.
  • Checkout abandonment - someone began checkout, started entering shipping and payment details, and quit. These people had decided to buy. Their reasons are concrete, researchable, and mostly fixable - and every one of them is a nearly-closed sale walking out the door.

Mango Lane's split: 36,000 cart sessions, 19,800 leave at the cart (partly normal), 16,200 proceed, and 6,480 of those quit mid-checkout (mostly fixable). The 6,480 is the number this session is about.

What to ask "When you say abandonment, do you mean cart or checkout?" - the single question that redirects a panicked meeting. Then: "What is our checkout completion rate, and how does it compare to the ~45% typical?" Mango Lane's answer is 60% - strong - which changes what gets funded (a6 picks that thread up).
Self-studyWhy the big number still matters a little2 min read▶

"Mostly normal" is not "ignore it". Two honest uses of the cart abandonment number: watch its trend (a sudden jump means something changed - price display, shipping banner, a bug), and mine the just-browsing majority with low-cost nudges (saved carts, back-in-stock notes, a reminder that respects the shopper). What you should not do is set a target like "halve cart abandonment" - you would be declaring war on window shopping, and you would lose.

Part 2 · the research table worth memorizing

Why checkouts die 10 min read

Baymard Institute surveys shoppers who began a checkout and quit, excluding the just-browsing crowd. The reasons are stable year over year and refreshingly concrete (Baymard Institute, 2025 survey wave; respondents could pick several):

LiveRead the table like a leader: policy beats pixels4 min▶

Scan the third column. The top killers are not design flaws - they are business decisions. Extra costs (40%) is your shipping fee policy. Slow delivery (20%) is your logistics contract. Account required (18%) is a management preference for "capturing" customers. Returns policy (13%) is commercial terms. Roughly half of checkout abandonment is set in your meetings, not in the design team's Figma files.

This reframes the classic escalation. When checkout completion disappoints, the reflex is "redesign the checkout". The research says: first check whether policy is doing the killing. A beautiful checkout that reveals a surprise $9.90 shipping fee at the last step will lose to an ugly one that said "free shipping" on the product page.

Real world

The cheapest checkout fix on record is a sentence. Stores that show the full cost early - shipping, tax, fees, on the cart or even the product page - attack the 40% reason and the 12% reason at once, with zero engineering. The expensive redesign can wait until the policy problems are gone.

What to ask "Of our checkout abandonment, how much is policy - shipping fees, delivery promise, forced accounts, returns terms - versus experience?" And: "When does a shopper first see the true total?" If the answer is "at the last step", you have found the 40% reason live in your own store.
Part 3 · shorter is not automatically better

Friction vs reassurance 10 min read

The lazy conclusion from Part 2 is "make checkout shorter". The research is more interesting. Baymard measures the average US checkout at 5.1 steps and 11.3 form fields shown, against an ideal of roughly 8 fields - and finds that field count matters more than step count. A five-step checkout with few, well-labeled fields per step outperforms a heroic one-page checkout crammed with everything. Meanwhile 19% of abandoners quit because they did not trust the site with card details - which means some additions (recognized payment logos, security cues, a human returns promise) raise completion. The craft is removing friction while adding reassurance.

LiveThe $260B claim3 min▶

Baymard's headline estimate: $260 billion of lost orders in the US and EU are recoverable through better checkout design alone - not more traffic, not lower prices, just fixing the flow and forms for people who already decided to buy. Treat the precise figure as an estimate, but take the shape of the claim seriously: the last mile is the highest-intent, lowest-cost place to recover revenue in ecommerce. Every session that reaches your checkout has already survived every earlier gate - losing it there wastes the entire journey you paid to create.

Self-studyGuest checkout: move the account ask to the receipt3 min read▶

"Site wanted an account created" kills 18% of checkout abandoners - shoppers holding a card, blocked by a registration form. The fix is not "no accounts"; it is sequencing. Let everyone check out as a guest, take the money, and then make account creation the receipt-page ask: "Save your details for next time?" After payment, the shopper has nothing left to lose and a reason to say yes (order tracking). Before payment, the same form is a wall. The information collected is identical - only the moment changes, and the moment is everything.

What to ask "Do we offer guest checkout, and where does the account ask happen - before the money or after?" Then: "How many fields does our checkout show, against the ~8-field ideal?" Both answers arrive in a day and often embarrass the current flow.
Demo · read along, no computation

Three checkout autopsies ★ 12 min · read along

Three ways a checkout number goes wrong, each diagnosed the way you would in a real review. Read the evidence, then the leader question that cracks each case.

Autopsy 1 - completion fell the week a promotion ended. Checkout completion slid from 60% toward 55% right after "free shipping over $80" expired. Nothing in the checkout changed - the total did. This is the 40% reason (extra costs) arriving on schedule: shoppers who built carts under free shipping now meet a fee at the pay step. A policy killed the number; no redesign will resurrect it. Leader question: "What changed in what the shopper pays or sees - price, shipping, fees - in the same week the number moved?"

Autopsy 2 - mobile completion runs 15 points under desktop. Same store, same prices, same policy - so the gap is experience. On a phone, 11.3 fields hurt roughly twice as much, and the absence of wallet payments (one-tap pay instead of typing a card number on glass) removes the strongest mobile shortcut. Leader question: "What is our completion rate split by device, and can a phone shopper pay without typing a card number?"

Autopsy 3 - the drop concentrates at the payment step. Shipping details complete fine; the loss piles up exactly where the card goes in. That pattern points at declined cards (10%), too few payment methods (9%), or a trust wobble (19%) - three different owners, one location. You cannot tell which from the completion rate alone; you need the per-step split. Leader question: "Show me completion step by step - shipping, payment, purchase. Where exactly do they quit?" (Your analysts build precisely this micro-funnel in b6 of the analyst track.)

The pattern across all three Never accept "checkout completion is down" as a finding. Ask when it moved (autopsy 1), for whom (autopsy 2), and at which step (autopsy 3). Three questions, no computation, and the room usually knows the answer to at least one of them already.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 20 min total

  • Buy something from your own store on your phone, with a fresh account (or no account). Count the steps, count the fields, and note every surprise - a fee you did not expect, a wall you had to climb.
  • Ask your team for your checkout completion rate against the ~45% typical benchmark, and whether that number is trending.
  • Ask for your top abandonment step - shipping, payment, or purchase - and which of the Baymard reasons your team believes owns it.
  • Bring one policy suspect (shipping fee, account wall, returns terms) to a6 - the prioritization session will teach you what it is worth.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Someone projects "70% of our carts are abandoned!" and the room panics. Your move?

Cart abandonment (70.22% average) is largely normal shopper behavior. Checkout abandonment - people who started paying and quit - is the smaller, mostly fixable number worth a meeting.

2 · The single biggest reason people abandon a checkout, per Baymard's 2025 survey wave?

Extra costs at 40% dwarfs everything else, and it is a policy decision. "Too long/complicated" sits at 17% - real, but a supporting actor. Half the fixes belong to leadership, not to pixels.

3 · Is a shorter checkout always a better checkout?

Baymard: ~8 fields is the ideal against 11.3 shown, and fields beat steps as the friction metric. Meanwhile reassurance - payment logos, security cues, clear returns - earns its space. Remove friction, add trust.
