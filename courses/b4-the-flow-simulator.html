Part 1 · covers the journey as a multiplicative system

The journey is one machine with four dials 10 min live

Mango Lane's month, as a flow: 360,000 sessions, 40% reach a PDP, 25% of those add to cart, 45% of those start checkout, 60% of those pay. Multiply through: 9,720 orders, 2.7% session CR, $660,960 GMV. Each rate is a dial - and because the flow is multiplicative, turning any single dial moves the end of the line by the same proportion.

LiveWhy fixes compound4 min▶

Gate improvements multiply, they do not add. Improve PDP penetration by 7.5% (40 to 43) and add-to-cart by 12% (25 to 28) and orders rise by roughly 1.075 x 1.12 = 20.4% - more than 7.5 + 12 would suggest for big moves. Four modest fixes below compound to +47% GMV. This is why journey work beats one-big-redesign work: several small, owned, testable gate deltas stack.

State every fix as a gate delta "Improve search" is a wish. "Move PDP penetration from 40% to 43% by fixing zero-result searches" is a fix - it names the gate, the size, and the mechanism. The simulator only accepts fixes in that form, and so should your roadmap.
Self-studyWhere the four fixes come from3 min read▶

The lever sizes in the simulator are illustrative teaching values, but each fix is a real, well-documented pattern:

  • Smarter search & nav - killing zero-result searches and improving autocomplete raises the share of sessions that ever reach a product page (b2-b3 showed search-path sessions convert about 2.7x browse-path).
  • Richer PDP - better galleries, visible reviews, and size guidance raise add-to-cart (b5 measures engaged-PDP sessions converting 2x plain ones).
  • Persistent cart + nudges - carts that survive across sessions and gentle reminders raise checkout starts.
  • One-page checkout + wallets - fewer form fields and wallet payments raise completion (b6 walks the checkout micro-funnel; Baymard's research catalogs exactly which frictions kill it).

Real world

This table is how journey roadmaps get funded. A UX team asking for "checkout redesign budget" gets questions; a team showing "completion 60% to 66% = +$88K GMV per month on current traffic" gets a decision. The simulator's honesty rail still applies - the effect sizes are estimates until an A/B test confirms them (that craft lives in learn-experimentation-with-phoebe) - but the arithmetic of the claim is exactly this.

Self-studyWhich fix first? The preview of a64 min read▶

All four rungs assume you can do everything at once. Real teams pick. Two useful tie-breakers, both computable from this machine:

  • Reach - how many sessions touch the gate. Gate 1 touches all 360,000 sessions; gate 4 touches only 16,200. A small % lift on a huge base can beat a big % lift on a tiny one.
  • Distance to benchmark - Mango Lane's completion (60%) is already strong versus the ~45% Shopify average (Littledata, 2025), while its PDP penetration has no such cushion. Fix the gate that lags its benchmark, not the one that is easiest to imagine.

a6 turns this into a full prioritization ritual (reach x severity x confidence); b7 uses the same machine backwards to diagnose a leak.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · PDP penetration improves 7.5% and add-to-cart improves 12%. Orders rise by...

Gate improvements compound multiplicatively: 1.075 x 1.12 = 1.204. For small moves the sum is a decent approximation; for stacked fixes the compounding is real money.

2 · What makes "improve search" unacceptable on a journey roadmap?

A fix must be a gate delta: "PDP penetration 40% to 43% by fixing zero-result searches." That form makes it valuable (the simulator prices it) and testable (an experiment can confirm it).

3 · Completion (60%) already beats the ~45% benchmark; PDP penetration has no such cushion. Which fix deserves scrutiny first?

Distance-to-benchmark plus reach are the two computable tie-breakers. A gate already beating its benchmark has less headroom; a gate touching all 360K sessions has more leverage per point.
